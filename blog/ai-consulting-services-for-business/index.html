What AI consulting actually delivers (roadmap, not slides forever)

A useful consulting engagement is narrow. Ours is built around deliverables you can hand to finance and IT — not a catalog of workshops that never become a SOW.

  • Problem statement: the job to be done, the system of record, and the person who owns the exception.
  • Constraints: data access, identity, compliance posture, and what must stay human.
  • First-workflow design: steps, tools, handoffs, and a definition of done you can test.
  • Commercial path: Discovery → Consulting → Starter → Growth, or a clear “not yet” if the inputs are missing.

What is out of scope until Starter is agreed: platform selection theater, multi-year roadmaps sold as implementation, and any claim that a pilot will “transform the business.” If you need an agent after the design is solid, that work sits on the agents track with a scoped Pay path — not inside an open consulting retainer that never ends.

Hire a partner when at least two of these are true: you have a real queue or process (not a wish list of “AI use cases”); internal teams can give access and a decision-maker but cannot spare a dedicated owner for weeks; you already tried a tool or pilot and it stalled at integration, governance, or adoption; you need a written scope and price path finance and IT can both accept. Do not hire a partner to “explore AI.” That is an internal workshop. Hire when you need someone accountable for a first production path — including the decision to stop if the workflow is not ready.

Outcomes-first prioritization

Companies buy AI tools before they buy clarity: no prioritized map across sales, ops, support, and finance; pilots without owners or success signals; confusion between chatbots, agents, and process automation; budgets stuck waiting for a “safe first step.” Outcomes-first consulting reverses that order.

Prioritization is impact × effort against a real operating signal you already care about — cycle time on a named queue, handoff completion on a named loop, hours returned on a named task. We will not invent that metric as a certified savings percentage on a slide. You measure after a baseline exists, during a defined evaluation window.

The map should answer three questions leadership can approve:

  1. What is the first workflow? One paragraph. One system of record. One exception owner.
  2. What must stay human? Irreversible actions, customer-facing commits, regulated decisions.
  3. What is the next commercial unit? Discovery (if still fuzzy), Consulting (if the blocker is a decision), Starter (if ready to build one slice), Growth (if ongoing ops), Consulte (if politics), or stop.

DIY is the right call when you already have a technical owner, access to the systems, and a single workflow you can ship without a committee. A partner is the right call when those pieces are split across teams — or when the risk is integration and operations, not model choice. The honest split is written down. If a vendor cannot tell you who does the work after week two, you are buying a slide deck. Service detail: AI consulting services.

When agents vs consulting first

Agents and consulting are not competitors. They are different units of work. Mixing them recreates the fog.

  • Consulting (or Discovery) first when three teams still debate which process; when chatbot vs agent vs RPA is unresolved; when the blocker is a roadmap, risk language, or leadership alignment; when nobody can name the exception owner. Start on AI consulting services or Discovery $99.
  • Agents first (or Starter on an agent job) when one agent job, one owner, approved tools, and stop rules are already written — and the question is build and operate, not “what even is the process?” Product framing: autonomous AI agents.
