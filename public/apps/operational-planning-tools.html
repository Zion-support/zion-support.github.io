Worked example: from assumptions to a review plan

This fictional scenario uses a 30-day month and USD for every monetary amount, with no currency conversion. It illustrates planning arithmetic, not customer results or a service commitment.

1. Establish the manual-work baseline

Assume 40 hours of manual work per month and a cost of USD 100 per hour.

40 hours/month × USD 100/hour = USD 4,000/month.

This is the current workload cost, not savings. Before claiming an improvement, measure the pilot's actual effort, implementation cost and quality against this baseline.

Check the manual-cost calculation

2. Separate downtime from illustrative credits

Assume a 99.9% availability target over 30 days (720 hours), one hour of downtime, a USD 1,000 period fee and a linear credit of 5% of that fee per excess hour.

Allowed downtime = 720 × (1 − 99.9/100) = 0.72 hours.

Excess downtime = max(0, 1 − 0.72) = 0.28 hours.

Credit per excess hour = USD 1,000 × 5/100 = USD 50/hour.

Illustrative total = 0.28 hours × USD 50/hour = USD 14.

The illustration applies no contractual caps, tiers or exclusions. Check the agreement and incident evidence before determining whether any credit is owed.

Check the SLA scenario

3. Turn unchecked controls into evidence questions

Assume six of ten governance controls are self-reported as present. The four unchecked controls become four evidence gaps to review.

10 controls − 6 self-reported controls = 4 evidence gaps.

Six checked boxes do not prove compliance or authorize a rollout. Assign owners to the four gaps and verify evidence for all ten controls, including those already checked.
